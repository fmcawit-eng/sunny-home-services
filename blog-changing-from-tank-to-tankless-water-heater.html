Changing from Tank to Tankless Water Heater: Full Guide

Book Now All Articles

Changing from a tank to a tankless water heater is more than a simple swap. Tankless units heat water differently, so your gas line, venting, and a few other components need to change. While it’s a major switch, many Wasatch Front homes benefit from lower energy bills, more space, and hot water that never runs out. In this guide, we’ll cover why homeowners make the switch, when it makes sense, what the installation involves, and the main cost factors, so you know exactly what to expect before scheduling.

Key Takeaways

  • Switching from a tank to a tankless water heater usually means upgrading the gas line and venting, plus adding an outlet and condensate drain.
  • If your tank water heater is over 10 years old, runs out of hot water regularly, or rusts around the base, it may be time to consider an upgrade.
  • The unit size and efficiency, gas line work, location, add-ons, and permits all affect the cost of a tankless conversion.
  • Sunny Home Services provides tank-to-tankless water heater conversions designed for Wasatch Front winters and Utah’s hard water.

Why Do Homeowners Replace a Tank Water Heater with Tankless?

Homeowners often replace a tank water heater with a tankless model for on-demand hot water, better efficiency, and a longer-lasting system. Water heating accounts for about 18% of a standard home’s energy use. Tankless water heaters are 24% to 24% more efficient than tank models in homes that use 41 gallons or less of hot water a day, which lowers your monthly utility bills. Even busy households that use around 86 gallons or more a day can still see 8% to 14% more efficiency by switching. A few other common reasons homeowners replace their systems include:

  • More space: A wall-mounted unit frees up utility closets or basement corners where a tank once sat.
  • Longer life: Most tankless units last over 20 years with routine professional maintenance.
  • Lower leak risk: With no 50-gallon tank sitting in your basement, there’s less risk of a tank flooding your space.

When Should You Switch to Tankless Water Heaters?

If your current tank is eight to 10 years old or showing signs of wear, it’s worth weighing whether to switch to a tankless water heater. Planning a conversion before your current system fails gives you time to compare your options and schedule the work on your timeline. It’s a good time to make the move if:

  • Your tank is nearing the 10-year mark.
  • You see rust-colored water or moisture around the base.
  • You hear popping or rumbling sounds.
  • Your family regularly runs out of hot water.
  • You’re finishing a basement or remodeling.

If your tank is only a few years old and working well, you don’t need to rush into a replacement. Waiting until your system nears the end of its lifespan lets you hold off on the investment and budget for the conversion on your own schedule.

What Changing to a Tankless Water Heater Involves

Changing to a tankless water heater involves sizing the new unit, upgrading gas and venting, adding power and drainage, and mounting and testing the system. Most conversions take about a day, unless your home needs a long gas line run or a new vent route. Here are the standard tankless water heater installation steps:

  • Step one: Size the unit. Your technician adds up how many fixtures you run at once and checks your incoming water temperature. Wasatch Front groundwater can drop into the 40s in winter, so the heater must raise the temperature to 70 degrees or more. That means Utah homeowners need a unit sized for winter flow rates instead of the average warm-climate standard.
  • Step two: Upgrade the gas line. A tank water heater burns around 40,000 BTU, while tankless units can need up to 199,000 BTU. Many homes require gas line upgrades to fuel the new unit.
  • Step three: Replace the venting. High-efficiency tankless units can’t use the same metal flue as your tank. They instead vent through PVC or a dedicated pipe that runs out of a side wall.
  • Step four: Add the power and drain. Both condensing and non-condensing gas models need a 120-volt outlet to power the system. Condensing units also need a small drain line for the moisture they produce.
  • Step five: Mount and test the unit. Technicians mount tankless heaters on the wall with isolation valves so you can flush them each year. They then test, permit, and inspect it to confirm everything meets code.

Not sure where to start? A technician can look at it the same day and tell you what it needs.

Cost Factors When Converting from Tank to Tankless Water Heaters

The biggest cost factors when converting from a tank to a tankless water heater are the unit itself and how much of your home’s gas, venting, and plumbing needs to change. Most tank-to-tankless water heater conversions cost somewhere between $6,500 and $8,500, with the unit making up around half of that. The top factors that affect your final price include:

  • Unit size and efficiency: Larger, condensing models cost more but waste less gas.
  • Gas line work: Installing a larger line or a long run from the meter adds labor and materials.
  • Location changes: Moving the water heater from a basement corner to an exterior wall means rerouting water lines and adding new venting.
  • Water treatment: Installing a softener or scale filter protects your water heater from hard water, which runs through Salt Lake, Utah, Davis, and Weber counties.
  • Permits and inspection: Utah’s tankless water heater requirements involve permits and inspections to confirm that the work meets code.

Upgrading to a gas tankless water heater may qualify you for an Enbridge Gas ThermWise rebate, which can lower your upfront costs. When you choose a local company like Sunny Home Services, your technician can walk you through the available incentives and the paperwork that comes with them.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a tank-to-tankless water heater conversion take?
Most tank-to-tankless conversions only take one day. Homes that need a long gas line run or a new vent route may take a little longer.
Do tankless water heaters keep up during Utah winters?
A properly sized tankless unit will keep up all winter. Colder incoming water reduces flow, so your technician will size the unit for the coldest groundwater temperatures.
How often do tankless water heaters need maintenance?
Annual water heater maintenance is standard for most Wasatch Front homes. Utah’s hard water leaves scale inside the heat exchanger, and a yearly flush keeps your unit running efficiently and protects your warranty.
